RADSIM

Physics-based radiography and CT — position, expose, reconstruct

Hi! Welcome to RadSim.

This program simulates real x-ray interactions behind realistic user interfaces. I wanted access to a software suite like this while going through a Radiation Technology program, but the product either did not exist or was prohibitively expensive. Here you can test adaptations, get familiar with the concepts, and play with the effects of ionising radiation without any of it landing on a patient.

Everything you image is a real voxel volume segmented from CT data, and the picture is made by casting rays through it. Nothing is drawn or faked: change the kV and the contrast changes because the photoelectric effect falls away, not because a slider told it to.

Some housekeeping

  • This project was built substantially with AI-assisted coding (Claude Code). Getting it to where it is now would have taken me years; instead it took days. Some features may not be fully functional because the AI believed they were working correctly. Take the simulations with a grain of salt.
  • There are two compute engines. Browser mode runs on your computer's CPU and is what you get if you are using this online. It computes quickly but the images are coarser. If you have a dedicated GPU you can download and run the software locally, which unlocks the Python GPU engine and with it much larger, much more detailed simulations. A few features stay locked in browser mode simply because they would take tens of minutes to compute otherwise — the contrast injector is the main one, so it falls back to a fixed preset timeline you can watch but not reprogram.
  • The EI and DI values can be finicky. With no real machine to benchmark against they do not carry much weight. Manual chest technique reads about right; lateral chest, hand and knee still read a few DI high, and I am fairly sure that is the technique chart against the phantom rather than the exposure index itself. Still working on it.
  • Some of these interfaces are reconstructed from my own memory of using them. I have no recorded video or photographs to work from, so there will be inaccuracies. If you see me in person, tell me what I got wrong.
  • This is a teaching tool, not a clinical one. Nothing here should be used to set real technique, estimate real dose, or make any decision about a real patient.

If you are new, start with a mode's Tutorial button rather than Open — it walks the whole workflow control by control and explains what each one does to the beam and to the image.

Enjoy. Working on this has ironed out a few of my own misunderstandings, so hopefully it does the same for you.
—Matt—

X-ray
Projection radiography — AEC, grids, technique
Position the subject, set the technique or let the AEC chambers meter it, and expose. Polyenergetic ray-casting through real voxel anatomy, with scatter, grids, beam hardening and an exposure index you can miss.
CT
Scout, plan, acquire, reconstruct
Zero the table, take scouts, plan scan groups and recons, then watch the gantry acquire. Filtered back-projection with contrast timing and bolus tracking on the console.
Model Editor
Paint voxel phantoms slice by slice
Build or alter a phantom directly: paint materials onto transverse slices with a live 3D preview, then save it as a subject the scanners can image.
Desktop only — slice painting wants a wide screen and a fine pointer.
Fluoroscopy
Live screening — a beam you drive with your foot
Pulsed screening on a GE OEC C-arm: hold the pedal, pick a pulse rate, and trade dose for noise in real time. Last-image hold, beam-on timer, and a C-arm you orbit around the patient.
Mammography
Soft-tissue contrast at 26–32 kV
Target/filter combinations, compression that visibly earns its contrast and dose, an AEC tuned for a beam an order of magnitude softer than radiography's, and CC / MLO views of a high-resolution breast phantom.
In development
Bone Mineral Density
DXA — two energies, one answer
Dual-energy absorptiometry of the spine and hip: two beams, a basis-material decomposition, and a T-score at the end of it.
Ultrasound
B-mode, M-mode & colour Doppler
No ionising radiation at all: a probe on the skin and pulse-echo imaging through the same voxel anatomy. Speckle, acoustic shadowing and post-cystic enhancement are not drawn — they fall out of one scanline march, the way they do in a real beam. The heart beats, M-mode measures it, and colour is one dot product with the flow.

Six gains stacked by depth, added to the machine's own ramp. Slide them apart and the image bands — the classic mis-set screen. Centre them and it comes back uniform, without a single number of the physics changing. Push the far ones hard and you amplify the receiver's noise instead of an echo.

Motion & M-mode
M cursor where the line cuts
Heart rate
72 bpm
The anatomy moves, not the hand: the probe holds its seat and the patient breathes and beats underneath it — which is why the speckle rides along with the tissue instead of sitting still in the screen. M-mode plots the one cursor line against time, six seconds a screen, one gridline a second. Count the gridlines between beats and you have measured the rate off the image.

Colour is the component of flow along the beam, so a vessel crossed at 90° shows nothing however fast it runs — rock the probe and it appears. Red toward, blue away. Drop the scale and the fast middle of a vessel wraps to the opposite colour: that is aliasing, and it is the same phase wrap as anywhere else. The box costs frame rate because the machine fires an ensemble of pulses inside it instead of one.

Compression is not optional: thinner tissue means less scatter, less dose, less motion and less overlap — the image gets better as the paddle gets lower. The force readout is the number the technologist watches.
Subject
Scattered (BI-RADS c) and heterogeneously DENSE (d) breasts carry the same seeded findings — density is what changes whether you can see them. The QC slab is the accreditation phantom: score the fibres, speck groups and masses you can count, hardest first.

The findings are not in the phantom — each case plants its own, so A–E are cases you have not seen and some of them are NORMAL. Read the image first, then reveal. Demo carries one of each finding.
